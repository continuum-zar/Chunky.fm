How chunky.fm works

chunky.fm is a live internet radio station where everyone listening hears the same second of the same song. One person chooses the records. This page is how that is actually done, for anybody who wants to know before they tune in.

Nothing is streamed

The obvious way to put thirty people inside the same second is to send them all one stream. This does the opposite. Each browser fetches the recording and plays it itself, so what crosses the network is a file, once, and not a sound in progress. Nobody's audio passes through the server while the session is running.

Which leaves the actual problem: thirty browsers each playing the same file, and no reason any two of them should be in the same place in it.

Every browser works out how wrong its own clock is

A computer's clock is usually wrong by some seconds, and every decision here is made against a timestamp from the station. So before a browser can align to anything it has to know the gap between its clock and the station's.

It measures the gap the way the Network Time Protocol does: send a probe, note when it left and when the reply came back, and assume the trip took the same time in each direction. Five probes go out, a hundred and fifty milliseconds apart so they don't queue behind each other, and the fastest round trip wins rather than the average — the quickest reply is the one least contaminated by a delay that had nothing to do with distance. The measurement is repeated every thirty seconds, because the browser's own clock drifts too.

Staying together is a different problem from starting together

Starting in the right place is arithmetic. Staying there is not: the clock that paces audio playback runs slightly fast or slow against the clock that tells the time, so a browser that was exactly right a minute ago is measurably off now.

The correction is deliberately not a seek. Seeking is audible — it is a gap and then a jolt, and doing it every minute would make the station unlistenable. Instead the playback speed is nudged, by at most two percent in either direction, until the error is gone. Browsers preserve pitch when playback rate changes (preservesPitch is the property, and it defaults to on), so two percent time-stretches the music instead of transposing it, and at two percent there is nothing to hear.

Under fifty milliseconds of error nothing happens at all, because correcting that would be fidgeting. Over a full second something has gone wrong enough that waiting for a two percent nudge would take minutes, so it seeks and accepts the glitch.

The room, and the microphone in it

There is a chat, and a way to ask for a record. Whoever is on the decks has a microphone they can talk over the music with, and a listener can put a hand up and be brought on air to say something themselves — voice goes over WebRTC, peer to peer, which is why it arrives in step with the record rather than a few seconds behind it.

Sessions end

A session is a set of records or a conversation, decided when the station goes on and said on the page while it is: an empty deck during a set is a gap between songs, and during a conversation it is the programme.

The station is off air between them, on purpose, and the front page says when the next one is. Some songs don't need better recommendations so much as better introductions, and an introduction needs somebody there to hear it.

Questions

How does everyone hear the same second of the same song?

Nothing is streamed. Each browser fetches the recording and plays it itself, then works out how far its own clock sits from the station's and starts at the position the station says it is at. It keeps measuring while the song plays, and corrects by changing playback speed by up to two percent rather than by seeking.

Why can't I just play these songs myself tomorrow?

You can. What you cannot do alone is hear a room react to a record at the moment it lands. The songs are the same; hearing them at the same instant as everyone else, with whoever chose them talking over the intro, is the part that does not survive being played back later.

Do I need an account to listen to chunky.fm?

No. There is no account and nothing to sign up for. You pick a name when you arrive and it is kept in your own browser.

Is chunky.fm free?

Yes. Listening is free and there is nothing to pay for.

Why is the station off air most of the time?

Because a session everyone turns up to is the whole point. Sessions happen on announced evenings and end when they end. When the station is off air, the front page says when the next one is.

Can I request a song or talk on air?

Both. There is a chat and a way to ask for something, and whoever is on the decks can talk over the music. A listener can put a hand up and be brought on air to say something themselves.

Is chunky.fm only music?

No. A session is either a set of records or a conversation, and the station says which one it is running. A conversation is on the same shared clock as the music: a guest is brought up onto the air and everyone hears the question and the answer at the same second, with the room able to ask for the mic itself. Nothing about the machinery above changes for one; what changes is what an empty deck means, which is why the page is told which kind of night it is rather than left to guess from the silence.
